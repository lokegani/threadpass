The problem

Compliance is checked periodically. Discharge is not.

Four gaps, and the last one has a deadline attached.

Checked, not monitored

Periodic manual sampling is straightforward to time a discharge around. Between visits there is no coverage at all.

Traced to the plant, not the unit

Shared treatment plants test the combined final outlet. A spike there cannot be traced back to any one unit — which fails the innocent units as much as it shields the responsible one.

Enterprise tools, enterprise prices

Continuous effluent monitoring already exists. It is priced and engineered for large plants, not for a small dyeing unit's budget.

A market-access deadline

Units that cannot produce supplier-level compliance data risk losing export orders. For a major employment sector, that is a livelihood problem as much as an environmental one.

How it works

Two layers, chosen because they fail differently

Cloud cover blinds the satellite but not the sensor. A sensor that is unplugged, fouled or bypassed says nothing about the river the satellite is still watching.

Layer 1 — satellite

Wide-area, and impossible to schedule around

A published water-quality index (NDTI) is computed from free Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery over the river stretch receiving the unit's discharge.

It covers the whole stretch continuously, and cannot be evaded by timing a discharge around an inspection — because there is no inspection to time it around.

Layer 2 — ground sensor

Attributable to one specific unit

A low-cost node at the unit's own effluent outlet logs pH, total dissolved solids and turbidity.

This supplies the one thing satellite imagery cannot: a reading that belongs to this unit rather than to the river in general. A deployed node costs roughly ₹7,000–17,400.

The rule that decides everything

Each channel is scored against that unit's own 30-day median, using a robust threshold that a discharge episode cannot inflate. Only sustained runs count. Then the two layers are compared.

Flagged — a verified violation

A satellite excursion and a ground-sensor excursion overlapping in the same time window. Certification is withheld or revoked.

Watch — disclosed, never a violation

One layer only. It could be an upstream discharge that was never this unit's, or cloud cover over the river. Recorded and shown to the buyer — but it does not affect eligibility.

Requiring both layers to agree is what makes a Flagged event worth acting on. It is equally what protects a compliant unit from being penalised for a discharge that was never theirs.

What it produces

Three things a buyer can actually use

Certificate of effluent compliance

One page. Certificate ID, the period covered, verified violations and watch observations stated separately, and a QR code that resolves to the public verification page.

Buyer due-diligence report

Three pages. A plain-English summary, both monitoring layers charted with every excursion marked, and a complete event log — each entry with its resolution recorded.

Public verification page

No account needed. Confirms whether a certificate is genuine and still valid, and reflects any later revocation — so a withdrawn certificate cannot keep being presented as current.

Who it is for

Two customers, and the unit is not the one paying

The buyer pays, first and structurally — not the MSME unit, and not government.

For the MSME dyeing unit

  • A record you hold. Framed as self-monitoring you choose to show a buyer — protection, not surveillance.
  • Evidence when you are compliant. Closure notices and lost orders on paperwork grounds are a real risk even where treatment practice is sound.
  • Within reach. ₹7,000–17,400 per node, with no per-unit cost for the satellite layer.
  • Watch events do not punish you. An uncorroborated reading is disclosed, not counted against you.

For the export-facing buyer

  • Supplier-level environmental data in a consistent, structured format rather than ad-hoc self-reported paperwork.
  • Coverage between audits. A continuous record in the gaps where site visits see nothing.
  • Independent verification. Scan the certificate; the public page confirms it, including if it has since been revoked.
  • Onboard a supplier base through an existing relationship rather than unit-by-unit cold outreach.
Stated plainly

What this does not claim

A compliance record that overstates itself is worth less than one that does not, so these are on the front page rather than in a footnote.

Scope and status

Cluster and outlet-level monitoring, not pipe-level forensic proof. It will not establish which of two adjacent units released a given litre of effluent, and it is not designed to.

Not a regulatory or statutory certification. It confers no legal compliance status. It sits alongside the existing regulatory framework rather than replacing or duplicating it.

A water-quality and compliance tool, not a climate technology, and not presented as one. The genuine indirect link is that enforcing existing Zero Liquid Discharge and effluent-reuse mandates depends on reliable monitoring, which this supports.

Phase 0 was run against three years of already-published government water-quality data for a Noyyal-basin station, rather than against assumed figures. The result was mixed, and is reported as mixed.

No anchor buyer has signed on yet. The go-to-market route through an export-facing buyer is a plan with sector precedent, not evidence that it is already working.

And this build in particular is a demonstration. Every reading on this site is simulated and the unit names are fictional. What is real is the method — the baseline, the threshold, the sustained-run rule and the dual-layer correlation all run exactly as described, on generated data.
